Project Goal:¶

Our goal is to help choose useful measurements for an early clinical trial of a new FSHD treatment. Our main question is: How does MRI fat fraction change over time, and how can it be used alongside strength and movement tests to track FSHD progression? Evaluating the usefulness of a measurement can help us detect changes in disease progression and determine whether a therapy is benefiting the patient.

To begin answering this question, we analyzed MRI images of the left tibialis anterior from one participant at two visits (TP1 and TP2). We calculated fat fraction at eight locations along the muscle and compared the results between visits. This helps us explore fat fraction as a measure of changes in muscle composition.

Disease Background¶

  • Prevalence and incidence: Facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD) is a disease characterized by muscle weakness. Signs and symptoms usually appear during adolescence, but the age of onset and severity vary widely. According to the National Library of Medicine, approximately 1 in 20,000 people have FSHD; about 95% of cases are FSHD1, and the remaining 5% are FSHD2. DelveInsight estimated that 33,500 people were living with FSHD in the United States in 2021. These are prevalence estimates. A Dutch study estimated an annual incidence of approximately 3 newly diagnosed cases per million people (Deenen et al., 2014; DelveInsight, 2025; National Library of Medicine, n.d.-a).

  • Symptoms: FSHD often affects the face, shoulders, and upper arms, but it can also affect the legs and other muscles. People may have difficulty lifting their arms, closing their eyes, smiling, walking, or climbing stairs. Weakness may be more severe on one side than the other. Weak shoulder muscles can also cause the shoulder blades to stick out. Additional symptoms may include hearing loss and retinal abnormalities that can affect vision (National Library of Medicine, n.d.-a).

  • Diagnosis: Doctors examine the pattern of muscle weakness and use genetic testing to help confirm FSHD. MRI can show changes within a muscle, but an MRI fat-fraction value alone does not diagnose the disease. Doctors consider symptoms, family history, and test results and may perform additional tests to rule out other conditions (Preston & Wang, 2025).

  • Current care: Treatment focuses on managing symptoms and helping people maintain independence. Physical therapy, appropriate exercise, pain management, and braces for foot weakness can help with movement and daily activities. Some patients need breathing support, while selected patients may benefit from surgery to stabilize the shoulder blades. These approaches address the effects of the disease rather than its underlying genetic cause (Preston & Wang, 2025).

  • Progression and prognosis: FSHD is a slowly progressing disease, but its severity and rate of progression vary among patients. For some, it may cause only mild muscle weakness, while others may lose the ability to walk due to FSHD. Generally, FSHD does not affect life expectancy, although complications involving breathing can occur in some cases. Since the disease is lifelong and progresses slowly, it is important to evaluate changes over an extended period using multiple measurements (National Library of Medicine, n.d.-b).

  • Biological mechanisms: FSHD involves abnormal activity of the DUX4 gene in skeletal muscle. In FSHD1, a shortened region of repetitive DNA called D4Z4 allows DUX4 to become active when it would normally be silenced. This abnormal activity contributes to muscle damage and weakness, leading to muscle atrophy, inflammation, and the replacement of muscle tissue with fat and scar tissue (National Library of Medicine, n.d.-a).

  • Clinical trials and future treatments: Current research is focused on addressing the underlying causes of FSHD. One example is delpacibart braxlosiran (del-brax) from Novartis, which belongs to a new class of RNA therapeutics and has shown potential to become the first disease-modifying treatment for FSHD. It has met its primary biomarker endpoint in Phase I/II clinical trials, and Phase III is currently underway. Del-brax uses an antibody oligonucleotide conjugate to deliver small interfering RNA to muscle cells, where it is intended to suppress DUX4 expression (Novartis, 2026).

Data Set¶

This project uses an FSHD dataset provided for BME 2315 that includes MRI measurements, clinical assessments, and functional measurements related to disease progression. The clinical dataset is derived from the research published by Wong et al. (2024), titled Regional and bilateral MRI and gene signatures in facioscapulohumeral dystrophy: implications for clinical trial design and mechanisms of disease progression, in the journal Human Molecular Genetics. The study investigated MRI measurements, gene expression signatures, and clinical characteristics associated with FSHD to better understand disease progression and inform clinical trial design.

The dataset provided for this project, Module2_FSHD_Dataset.csv, contains information from 34 participants, including demographic information and measurements collected at baseline, year 1, and year 2. The measurements were collected at different intervals for each participant, and not every measurement is available at every time point. The dataset allows us to investigate changes in disease-related measurements over time and compare their potential usefulness as exploratory endpoints in FSHD clinical trials.

For the MRI methodology analysis, we focus on subject UW_0020 and MRI images of the left tibialis anterior (TA) muscle. The provided MRI dataset contains images from two time points, TP1 and TP2, allowing muscle composition to be examined at different visits. The tibialis anterior is located in the anterior compartment of the lower leg and contributes to ankle dorsiflexion.

The MRI data include separate images representing water and fat signals, stored as NumPy (.npy) files. These images contain MRI signal intensities associated with water and fat within the imaged tissue. The signals are used to calculate fat fraction, which estimates the proportion of the combined water and fat signal attributable to fat. Fat fraction is expressed as a percentage, while the original image intensities represent relative signal values rather than absolute physical concentrations.

The dataset also includes segmentation masks identifying the left tibialis anterior muscle. Each mask distinguishes pixels belonging to the muscle from surrounding tissues. These masks allow fat fraction to be calculated specifically within the tibialis anterior rather than across the entire MRI image.

MRI images were provided at eight sampled locations along the muscle for each time point. The locations are expressed as percentages of muscle length, allowing measurements from different positions to be compared. The accompanying slice information file identifies the water, fat, and mask files associated with each location.

The clinical dataset includes MRI fat fraction, fat fraction variability, and STIR measurements, as well as DUX4 gene expression signatures, D4Z4 repeat information, quantitative muscle strength measurements, and functional assessments. Functional measurements include Timed Up and Go, walking and running performance, and stair ascent and descent. Clinical severity scores are also provided.

The MRI images and segmentation masks were supplied as part of the BME 2315 course materials. The files were provided in a format that allows the water and fat images to be loaded directly into Python and the segmentation masks to be applied during analysis. The original image acquisition and segmentation procedures were not performed as part of this project. Instead, we use the supplied images and masks to reproduce the calculation of tibialis anterior fat fraction.

MRI Data Analysis Methodology¶

MRI Analysis Overview¶

The purpose of this analysis is to calculate the fat fraction of the tibialis anterior muscle using MRI water and fat images. Fat fraction provides a measurement of muscle composition by estimating the proportion of fat signal relative to the combined water and fat signal. This measurement is relevant to FSHD because progressive muscle deterioration can result in the replacement of muscle tissue with fat.

We will use Python to load the MRI images and segmentation masks provided in the dataset. The segmentation masks will allow us to isolate the tibialis anterior muscle from surrounding tissues. We will calculate fat fraction for the entire supplied image region and for the tibialis anterior muscle specifically. We will then visualize the images, examine the distribution of fat fraction values, and calculate summary statistics.

Finally, we will compare fat fraction measurements across different locations along the muscle and between the two available MRI time points, TP1 and TP2. This will allow us to investigate how muscle composition varies by location and visit.

Calculating Fat Fraction¶

Fat fraction is calculated by dividing the fat signal intensity by the combined fat and water signal intensities. This calculation produces a percentage representing the proportion of the total signal associated with fat. To avoid division by zero, pixels where the combined water and fat signal is zero are excluded from the calculation. The segmentation mask is then used to isolate fat fraction values within the tibialis anterior muscle.

In [3]:
# Calculate the combined water and fat signal
total_signal = water + fat

# Calculate fat fraction while avoiding division by zero
fat_fraction = np.divide(
    fat,
    total_signal,
    out=np.zeros_like(fat, dtype=float),
    where=total_signal > 0
) * 100

# Extract fat fraction values within the tibialis anterior
muscle_fat_fraction = fat_fraction[muscle_mask]

# Calculate summary statistics
mean_ff = np.mean(muscle_fat_fraction)
median_ff = np.median(muscle_fat_fraction)
std_ff = np.std(muscle_fat_fraction)

Interpolation Analysis:¶

Interpolation of Tibialis Anterior Fat Fraction¶

The MRI dataset contains fat fraction measurements at eight locations along the tibialis anterior muscle, ranging from 10% to 80% of muscle length in increments of 10%. However, fat fraction values are not directly measured at locations between these sampled points.

To estimate fat fraction at an unsampled location, we will use linear interpolation. This method estimates a value between two known measurements by assuming that fat fraction changes at a constant rate between those locations.

For this analysis, we will estimate the mean fat fraction at 45% of muscle length using the measured values at 40% and 50%. We will perform this calculation for both TP1 and TP2 to compare the estimated fat fraction between visits.

Because 45% falls between two measured locations, this calculation is interpolation rather than extrapolation.

Interpretation of Interpolation Results¶

Linear interpolation was used to estimate the mean fat fraction at 45% of the tibialis anterior muscle length. The estimated fat fraction was 25.40% at TP1 and 46.30% at TP2, representing a difference of 20.90 percentage points between the two visits.

The estimates were calculated using the measured mean fat fraction values at 40% and 50% of muscle length. Because 45% is halfway between these sampled locations, linear interpolation estimates the fat fraction by taking the average of the two neighboring measurements.

The interpolation method assumes that fat fraction changes at a constant rate between the sampled locations. However, the actual distribution of fat within the muscle may not follow a straight line. Therefore, the interpolated values are estimates rather than new MRI measurements.

Both estimates fall within the range of the sampled locations, meaning interpolation was used rather than extrapolation. Extrapolation would involve estimating values outside the measured range, where predictions would generally be more uncertain.

Several factors could affect the accuracy of these estimates, including differences in MRI acquisition, segmentation accuracy, and variation in muscle composition between sampled locations. Because no MRI measurement was collected at exactly 45% of muscle length, we cannot determine the accuracy of the interpolated estimates without additional measurements.

The higher estimated fat fraction at TP2 is consistent with the overall increase observed between visits. However, additional measurements would be needed to determine whether this difference reliably reflects FSHD progression.

    ], dtype=float)

    # Remove missing measurements
    valid = np.isfinite(times) & np.isfinite(values)

    times = times[valid]
    values = values[valid]

    # At least two measurements are required
    if len(times) < 2:
        continue

    # Sort measurements chronologically
    order = np.argsort(times)
    times = times[order]
    values = values[order]

Comparison of Potential Clinical Trial Endpoints¶

To compare potential clinical trial endpoints, longitudinal changes were calculated for MRI fat fraction, dorsiflexion muscle strength, functional performance, and clinical severity. Measurements were linearly interpolated to common time points of 11.15 months (Year 1) and 22.13 months (Year 2). Mean relative percentage changes and standard deviations were then calculated across participants.

Among the measurements analyzed, the walking/running assessment (WR-10) demonstrated the lowest standard deviation of relative percentage change at both time points. Its standard deviation was 10.38% at Year 1 and 12.11% at Year 2, suggesting relatively consistent changes between participants. However, its mean changes were comparatively small, at +2.06% and +3.35%, respectively.

MRI fat fraction demonstrated larger mean relative changes, increasing by 6.05% at Year 1 and 10.30% at Year 2. Its standard deviations were 15.99% and 17.92%, respectively. These findings suggest that MRI fat fraction may be useful for detecting structural changes associated with FSHD progression, although the variation between participants remains important.

Dorsiflexion strength measurements demonstrated substantially greater variability. Standard deviations ranged from 27.63% to 41.43%, indicating that changes in muscle strength were less consistent across participants when expressed as relative percentages. Mean strength changes were positive, so these results did not demonstrate a consistent decline in strength over the observation period.

Other functional measurements, including Timed Up and Go and stair ascent and descent, showed relatively small mean changes and varying directions of change. This makes it difficult to identify a consistent pattern of functional decline using these measurements alone.

Overall, the results suggest that MRI fat fraction and WR-10 may be useful exploratory endpoints for future FSHD clinical trials. MRI fat fraction demonstrated larger average relative changes, while WR-10 demonstrated lower variability. Combining structural MRI measurements with functional assessments may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of disease progression than relying on a single measurement.

Conclusions¶

The goal of this project was to investigate how different measurements can be used to monitor disease progression in facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD) and identify potential exploratory endpoints for future clinical trials. Our analysis included MRI fat fraction measurements from a single participant's tibialis anterior muscle and longitudinal clinical data from a cohort of 34 participants. Together, these analyses provided insight into changes in muscle composition, muscle strength, and functional performance over time.

MRI Fat Fraction Analysis¶

Our MRI image analysis demonstrated an increase in fat fraction between two time points. The average fat fraction across eight sampled locations in the left tibialis anterior muscle increased from 42.26% at TP1 to 54.41% at TP2, representing an increase of 12.15 percentage points.

We also used linear interpolation to estimate fat fraction at 45% of the muscle length. At this location, fat fraction increased from 25.40% at TP1 to 46.30% at TP2, an increase of 20.90 percentage points. This difference demonstrates that changes in muscle composition may vary considerably depending on the location within the muscle. Therefore, consistent imaging locations and standardized measurement methods are important when comparing MRI measurements across visits.

Longitudinal Clinical Data Analysis¶

To evaluate disease progression across participants, we performed linear interpolation of longitudinal clinical measurements to common comparison times of 11.15 months (Year 1) and 22.13 months (Year 2). We then calculated mean relative percentage changes and standard deviations for MRI fat fraction, dorsiflexion strength, functional performance, and clinical severity.

Right-side MRI fat fraction demonstrated a mean relative increase of 6.05% at Year 1 and 10.30% at Year 2, with standard deviations of 15.99% and 17.92%, respectively. These results suggest that MRI fat fraction may be useful for detecting structural changes associated with disease progression. However, the standard deviations indicate that the magnitude of these changes varied between participants.

Among the functional measurements analyzed, the walking/running assessment (WR-10) demonstrated the lowest standard deviation of relative percentage change at both time points. Its standard deviation was 10.38% at Year 1 and 12.11% at Year 2, with mean relative changes of 2.06% and 3.35%, respectively. This suggests that WR-10 exhibited relatively consistent changes across participants, although the average changes were smaller than those observed for MRI fat fraction.

Dorsiflexion strength measurements demonstrated substantially greater variability, with standard deviations ranging from 27.63% to 41.43%. Additionally, the average strength measurements increased rather than decreased over time, which does not reflect the expected pattern of progressive muscle weakness. These findings may reflect differences between participants, measurement variability, or other factors that cannot be determined from the available data alone.

Other functional measurements, including Timed Up and Go and stair ascent and descent, demonstrated relatively small average changes and inconsistent directions of change. These findings suggest that functional measurements alone may not consistently capture disease progression over the observation period.

Recommendations for Future Clinical Trials¶

Based on our analysis, MRI fat fraction and WR-10 appear to be promising exploratory endpoints for future FSHD clinical trials, although for different reasons.

MRI fat fraction demonstrated relatively large average changes over time and provides a quantitative measurement of structural changes within skeletal muscle. This makes it potentially useful for detecting disease-related changes that may occur before noticeable differences in physical performance.

WR-10 demonstrated the lowest variability in relative percentage changes among the measurements analyzed. Although its average changes were smaller, functional assessments provide information about physical abilities that are directly relevant to patients' daily lives.

Therefore, combining MRI fat fraction with functional assessments such as WR-10 may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of FSHD progression than relying on either measurement alone. MRI can provide information about structural muscle changes, while functional measurements can help determine whether those changes are associated with meaningful differences in physical performance.

Additional Evidence Needed¶

Before these measurements could be used as reliable clinical trial endpoints, additional research would be necessary. Larger longitudinal studies would help determine whether the observed patterns remain consistent across a broader FSHD population.

It would also be important to evaluate the relationship between MRI fat fraction changes and functional outcomes, including muscle strength, walking performance, and clinical severity. Establishing these relationships could help determine whether changes in MRI measurements correspond to clinically meaningful changes in patients' abilities.

Additionally, studies involving treated and untreated participants would be necessary to determine whether these measurements can distinguish treatment effects from natural disease progression. Measurement reproducibility, differences between muscle groups, and the effects of missing data should also be investigated.

Ethical Considerations¶

Selecting clinical trial endpoints involves ethical considerations because the chosen measurements influence how treatment effectiveness is evaluated.

MRI provides detailed information about muscle composition, but it can be expensive and may not be equally accessible to all patients. Functional assessments may be more accessible and directly relevant to daily activities, but their results can be influenced by fatigue, motivation, and differences in physical ability.

Relying exclusively on MRI measurements could overlook improvements in strength or mobility that are meaningful to patients. Conversely, relying only on functional assessments could fail to detect early structural changes associated with disease progression.

For these reasons, clinical trials should consider multiple complementary measurements and prioritize outcomes that reflect both biological changes and improvements in patients' quality of life.

Overall Conclusion¶

Our findings suggest that MRI fat fraction is a promising measurement for monitoring structural changes associated with FSHD, while WR-10 demonstrated relatively consistent changes across participants. However, neither measurement alone provides a complete assessment of disease progression.

A combination of MRI, muscle strength, and functional assessments may provide a more comprehensive approach to evaluating FSHD progression and potential treatment effectiveness. Further research with larger participant groups, standardized measurement methods, and clinically meaningful outcomes is needed before determining which endpoints are most appropriate for future clinical trials.

Limitations and Future Work¶

Although our analysis provided insight into potential measurements of FSHD progression, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results.

Sample Size and Missing Data¶

One limitation of this study was the relatively small dataset of 34 participants. Although the dataset included multiple measurements of muscle composition, strength, and functional performance, not every participant had complete measurements at all three time points.

For example, only 32 participants had recorded Year 1 visit times, and 27 had recorded Year 2 visit times. In our longitudinal MRI fat fraction analysis, 31 participants had sufficient data for interpolation at Year 1, while only 26 had sufficient data at Year 2.

These missing measurements reduced the number of participants included in our calculations and may have influenced the observed averages and standard deviations. Additionally, differences in the participants included at each time point limit direct comparisons between Year 1 and Year 2 results.

Future studies should include larger participant groups and prioritize consistent follow-up measurements to reduce missing data and improve the reliability of longitudinal comparisons.

Limitations of Linear Interpolation¶

Our longitudinal analysis used linear interpolation to estimate measurements at common comparison times of 11.15 months and 22.13 months. This method allowed us to compare participants whose visits occurred at slightly different times.

However, linear interpolation assumes that measurements change at a constant rate between observed time points. FSHD progression may not occur linearly, and individual participants may experience periods of faster or slower progression.

Additionally, interpolation cannot reliably estimate measurements outside the observed time range without extrapolation. We avoided extrapolating beyond the available measurements, which resulted in some missing interpolated values.

Future analyses could compare linear interpolation with alternative approaches, such as quadratic interpolation or longitudinal statistical models, to determine which methods best represent changes over time. These approaches would require sufficient observations to avoid introducing additional uncertainty.

MRI Analysis Limitations¶

Our detailed MRI image analysis focused on the left tibialis anterior muscle of a single participant at two time points. Although we observed an increase in average fat fraction from 42.26% to 54.41%, this result cannot be generalized to the entire FSHD population.

We also observed that fat fraction varied across different locations within the muscle. Our spatial interpolation at 45% muscle length produced a larger increase than the average across all eight sampled locations, demonstrating that measurement location can influence the estimated degree of muscle change.

Additionally, our overall MRI averages were calculated by giving equal weight to each sampled muscle location rather than weighting measurements by muscle area or volume. This means the averages do not necessarily represent the true fat fraction of the entire muscle.

Future studies should examine MRI data from multiple participants and muscle groups. More comprehensive three-dimensional measurements, standardized imaging locations, and volume-weighted calculations could provide a more representative assessment of muscle composition.

Variability Between Participants¶

Our longitudinal analysis demonstrated considerable variability in disease-related measurements.

For example, MRI fat fraction showed mean relative increases of 6.05% at Year 1 and 10.30% at Year 2, but the corresponding standard deviations were 15.99% and 17.92%.

Dorsiflexion strength measurements demonstrated even greater variability, with standard deviations ranging from 27.63% to 41.43%.

These results indicate that participants did not experience identical changes over time. Differences in disease severity, affected muscle groups, individual progression rates, and measurement variability may contribute to these differences.

Future research could investigate whether factors such as age, baseline disease severity, and initial MRI fat fraction are associated with different rates of progression.

Limitations of Comparing Clinical Trial Endpoints¶

Our comparison of potential clinical trial endpoints was based primarily on mean relative percentage changes and their standard deviations.

Although WR-10 demonstrated the lowest standard deviation at both time points, lower variability does not necessarily indicate greater sensitivity to disease progression or treatment effects.

Relative percentage changes can also become unstable when baseline measurements are close to zero. Additionally, the measurements included in our analysis represent different aspects of FSHD and may not be directly comparable based on percentage change alone.

Our analysis did not establish whether changes in MRI fat fraction were associated with changes in muscle strength or functional performance. Therefore, we cannot determine whether the observed structural changes corresponded to clinically meaningful differences in participants' physical abilities.

Future analyses could examine correlations between MRI fat fraction and functional measurements, evaluate measurement reproducibility, and compare the ability of different endpoints to detect meaningful changes over time.

Future Research and Clinical Applications¶

Future studies should investigate whether combining structural MRI measurements with functional assessments provides a more reliable evaluation of FSHD progression than using individual measurements.

MRI fat fraction may help identify changes in muscle composition, while strength and functional assessments can provide information about physical performance and limitations in daily activities.

It would also be valuable to investigate whether baseline molecular measurements, such as DUX4 gene expression signatures, are associated with subsequent changes in MRI fat fraction or functional performance. Because DUX4 signatures were only available at baseline in our dataset, we could not evaluate their longitudinal changes.

Finally, studies comparing treated and untreated participants would be necessary to determine whether these measurements can distinguish treatment effects from natural disease progression.

Overall, addressing these limitations through larger datasets, standardized measurement methods, and more comprehensive longitudinal analyses could improve the identification of reliable and clinically meaningful endpoints for future FSHD clinical trials.
